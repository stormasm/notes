### A Week With Jensen Huang Just Before His Fall as Nvidia CEO

We traveled across Europe with the billionaire at the height of his power.

By Joshua Brustein, Emily Chang, and Ian King   
November 16, 2026 at 10:00 AM UTC

About a month before he lost his job as the chief executive officer of Nvidia Corp., Jensen Huang was in London, on the second day of a tour through Europe. That evening, Huang was the guest of honor at an event in a converted church, where he’d taken the stage to announce, in his best Oprah voice, that his company was giving capital to a handful of British artificial intelligence startups. “I’m investing in YOU!” Huang exclaimed. “I’m investing in YOU! I give YOU money! I give YOU money!”

With the official business of the night out of the way, Huang sauntered outside to greet a handful of reporters, holding a brightly colored drink garnished with a large slice of fruit. Men in suits, including a security guard with an earpiece, stood behind him as he took questions. “Do you guys have anything to drink?” Huang asked the scrum surrounding him, before taking two slugs from his own glass and letting out a satisfied sigh. “All I’ve had today is a quarter of a half of a sandwich,” he proclaimed. He smiled and added, “And two drinks. The prime minister and I had a nice whiskey together. He’s a whiskey man.”

Nvidia staffers had said that Huang would have about five minutes to answer questions. Instead he hung out for the better part of an hour, discussing a wide range of topics, waving to passersby, joking about how tipsy he was (“I thought it was lemonade”), and gracefully dealing with a heckler who shouted at him about Palestine. At one point, a female reporter asked a question about the relative lack of women who start tech companies. “If you started a company, I’d invest in you,” Huang responded. He noted that many amazing women worked at Nvidia, waxed romantic about his wife, Lori, for a moment, and then reached out and gave the woman a little fist bump.

At the time, there was nothing particularly notable about the appearance. Huang had long cultivated an image as someone who played it looser than other executives, mixing it up with crowds and skirting the line of acceptable public CEO behavior. It had served him well as Nvidia became a global power broker by enabling a technological shift that’s been compared with the Industrial Revolution, the discovery of fire and even the big bang. If a single person were to be crowned king of the AI boom, Huang would probably take the throne.

In October, Huang was on a multicity European tour, one of many such diplomatic missions that became a routine part of his job. Bloomberg Businessweek traveled with him, tagging along as he encouraged European policymakers to embrace AI, announced business deals, raffled off computers and took countless selfies with adoring nerds.

His tone during the trip was characteristically celebratory. But the clouds were already visible. Semiconductor shares were on their longest losing streak since the 2008 financial crisis, and Nvidia’s price would soon begin to slide from its peak. In the rush to build data centers, historically profitable tech giants Alphabet, Amazon.com, Meta Platforms and others were burning more cash than they were bringing in. Although Nvidia was still essentially printing money from chip sales, it had been making a spree of AI investments, including in companies that would turn around and buy Nvidia chips, that looked shaky. These so-called neoclouds, which borrow money to build data centers they rent out to others, were facing high interest rates and at times falling far behind schedule on construction. Given the financial entanglements between these interconnected businesses, concern was mounting that if the momentum slowed anywhere, trouble could cascade through the system.

Finances aside, the unpopularity of AI — and of data centers in particular — was developing into a major political issue. President Donald Trump’s embrace of the industry was shaping up to be a liability for his Republican Party in the US midterm elections. Huang had become close to the president, and it wasn’t clear how he’d fare if the US political environment shifted.

In the end, it wasn’t the things everyone could see coming that got to him. In late October, the New York Times reported that the US government was worried about Huang’s personal relationship with a woman who had founded a major Nvidia customer in Singapore, because officials suspected she was a Chinese spy. The Chinese government has denied she was involved in espionage, and her former employer Megaspeed International Pte. says it “conducts legitimate and lawful business.” Huang also brushed off the reports, saying there was nothing untoward about the relationship. It looked awkward for Huang, but manageable.

Then things began moving very quickly. Further revelations emerged, and Huang admitted to marital infidelity, apologized to his family and his colleagues, and said he would cooperate with Nvidia’s investigation into the possibility that he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. On Nov. 11, reports surfaced of another affair, this one with Nvidia’s vice president of healthcare, Kimberly Powell, who reported directly to Huang. Hours later the board gathered remotely to discuss Huang’s ouster. The next morning, he was out of a job.

Nvidia Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress took over as interim CEO while the company began its search for a permanent replacement. As of the end of last week, Nvidia had lost more than $1.8 trillion in market value from its high in early October, more than the market values of 495 of the companies on the S&P 500. With Huang gone, critical questions remain: What legal exposure do he and others at Nvidia face related to allegations of violating export controls? How much scrutiny is coming in Washington, where officials from both parties are sharpening their swords? Can Nvidia keep up its breakneck pace of development and ward off competitors who see an opening?

The crisis at Nvidia could also aggravate all the vulnerabilities facing the AI industry before Huang’s self-defenestration. Nvidia faces more significant competition on AI chips than it ever has, but its products are still critical to the industry, and any stumbles in its operations could reverberate widely. Huang was also personally engaged in Nvidia’s role as AI’s central bank, and his successor may not be inclined to spread the money around. Any pullback could cause investors to lose faith in companies they assumed had Nvidia’s long-lasting support.

When the smoke clears, l’affaire Huang may go down as the most consequential CEO scandal since the establishment of modern Silicon Valley — or maybe even since the discovery of fire.

For one of the world’s most important companies, the management at Nvidia had always been personal. Its org chart consisted of Huang at the top, with about 60 people reporting directly to him. All major decisions hinged on his approval, and Nvidians treated his judgments on strategic and technical matters as if they’d been handed down on stone tablets. The people who remain to reimagine the company will have to contend with a corporate culture they were immersed in themselves. “Jensen, I mean, come on, he’s a genius. OK? Come on, he’s just a genius,” Kress once told Businessweek. Like many people at Nvidia, she was particularly taken by the way Huang sketched out ideas on the whiteboards in the company’s office. “It’s artistic. Creepily artistic,” she said. “Truly, utterly impressive.”

Nvidia employees professed to be infatuated with Huang’s command over the myriad 
details of their work. In a 2023 interview with Businessweek, Powell recounted her first meeting with him, two weeks after joining Nvidia from a medical-imaging company to work on its healthcare and higher-education initiatives. At the time, these were microscopic parts of Nvidia’s business, and yet Huang showed what struck her as a deep interest, as he began looking for ways to fit it into the technologies the company was already working on. “Jensen can see the neural network and connection points probably across this company better than anybody,” she said, likening the company’s structure to the architecture of modern AI systems. (It isn’t clear when their alleged romantic relationship may have begun.)

At the same time that Huang’s appearance in every corner of Nvidia’s operations was part of his mystique, some people recognized it as a vulnerability too. In 2024, a former Nvidia executive told Businessweek that one of the biggest risks the company faced was the difficulty that anyone who wasn’t Huang would have running it. “His company is built for him,” this person said, asking to remain anonymous to avoid damaging professional relationships. “He built the glove to fit his hand.”

As Nvidia pursued the data center industry, Huang pushed to speed up its product development cycles, driving forward an efficient and hard-charging ethos that kept it ahead of the competition. But his legend was really cemented when he hit correctly on the biggest bet in the history of Silicon Valley. Huang and his two co-founders had started Nvidia as a producer of specialized chips for rendering visuals in video games, known as graphics processing units. He became convinced that GPUs could prove useful beyond the gaming world, and would be involved in applications such as weather forecasting and oil exploration. Because he held enough control over Nvidia to exert his will, he used this power to pursue these nonexistent markets even when it annoyed the company’s shareholders and staff, who worried he was losing focus.

Huang searched endlessly for an industry that could take full advantage of Nvidia’s GPUs. He recalled Elon Musk telling him that a nonprofit he’d helped start might be a good customer. Nonprofits weren’t exactly the kind of deep-pocketed client Huang was hoping for. Nonetheless he hand-delivered what Nvidia billed as “the world’s first AI supercomputer in a box” to OpenAI’s office in San Francisco in 2016.

GPUs, it turned out, were ideal for training the AI systems OpenAI was building. That organization and other AI pioneers built their tech specifically to run on Nvidia’s computing architecture, a task the company had made easier by writing software to help adapt the chips for purposes beyond graphics processing. As a result, Huang’s company had a huge head start in making the physical brains of modern AI. Nvidia went from niche equipment manufacturer to the most valuable company on Earth, overtaking Microsoft Corp. in June 2024 and inspiring a sense of loyalty among Huang’s fans that bordered on the cultish. “We love the guy,” the former Nvidia executive said. “He made us all super f---ing rich.”

There were plenty of unlovable aspects of Huang, though. He has a short fuse and could be cruel. In one instance, according to people familiar with the exchange who asked not to be identified discussing internal communications, Huang castigated a senior sales employee in a roomful of 40 people: “You don’t know dick about my business.” In a separate meeting this February, Jay Puri, then the company’s executive vice president for worldwide field operations, informed Huang that a conversation with an Nvidia customer didn’t go as planned. Puri happened to be nursing a cold that day, people familiar with the exchange say, and Huang expressed little sympathy. “I hope your cold gets worse,” Huang said. “I hope you get the flu. And I hope it gets worse. I hope you die and put all of us out of our misery.”

Huang installed allies on his board and engendered loyalty by working like a maniac and delivering stellar financial results, but in one key way his power was actually less formalized than the founders of Alphabet, Meta, SpaceX and similar companies, whose shares hold additional voting power that makes it essentially impossible for their boards to discipline them. “I serve at the pleasure of the board, and I serve at the pleasure of the shareholders,” Huang told Businessweek in a lengthy interview in October at the Lanesborough Hotel in London. “Unlike many CEOs, I’m completely fireable.”

Huang’s two adult children have been working at Nvidia for years, along with his son-in-law. His daughter, Madison, who went to culinary school and worked as a sommelier before getting an MBA and joining Nvidia, has occasionally been mentioned by colleagues as a potential successor. Madison was by her dad’s side every day of his recent European trip, effectively serving as his chief of staff. (At one point, Huang couldn’t remember the name of a company he wanted to mention to a group of people, and sent an aide to get Madison, who supplied the information.) But Huang bristled at the suggestion that Nvidia was a “family business.” Although he said he hoped Nvidia’s next CEO would be someone who already worked for the company, he added that his successor would have to earn the position, regardless of who they were.

“Just as I can be fired, the next CEO has to be hired,” he told Businessweek during the trip. “I like that we can’t be overly complacent about our position inside the company and that we can’t take it for granted. You’re always vulnerable, you’re always earning it, you’re always striving to be better.”

Huang always approached his role at the helm of his semiconductor design company as if it required the baby-kissing existence of a retail politician. When AI took off, his audience grew by orders of magnitude. Huang saw it as his duty to promote the technology even when the public and many of his peers were urging caution. He spent much of his time in recent years on the road touting the wonders of AI, inspiring a weirdly rapturous response from San Jose to Taipei.

It was also apparent to even casual observers that he loved the attention. At Nvidia events he personally gave away expensive computing equipment to fans; he also moderated panel discussions in which prominent figures in the tech industry explained how important Nvidia was to them. He was regularly mobbed in public.

Huang delighted in pushing the limits of decorum. When Nvidia’s general counsel suggested that drinking not be permitted at its campus, Huang overruled him, and then named an on-site pub after him. When he was on the road, Huang offered running commentary on his own public drinking, mocked people’s hard-to-pronounce names and riffed on American stereotypes of pushy French people and overly cautious Germans. At an event in Taiwan in 2024, he autographed a young woman’s shirt right across her chest. At another one, last year, he summoned Powell, the healthcare executive and his reported paramour, onstage to open a bottle of wine, so he could refill his drink.

Looking back, the signs were there that Huang might have been losing touch. He raised eyebrows by telling the New York Times in September that he couldn’t even remember his own address, an admission intended as commentary on services such as Google Maps that instead showed what can happen to people who rely too much on their chauffeurs. “I am super normal. I’m super, super normal,” Huang insisted in one of his conversations with Businessweek this fall, going through a list of things he still does for himself. “I make my own food. I wash my own dishes. I take care of my own dogs.”

Huang’s ease in the spotlight, his company’s substantial economic power and his relationship with Trump made him one of the most influential voices in the AI discourse. The president’s opinions on AI often overlapped with his own. Both men pushed back on calls for new domestic regulations, saying they risked slowing down the technology and asserting that the industry could be disciplined by a mixture of market forces and self-regulation. Trump also defied the China hawks in his administration and agreed to give Nvidia licenses to sell some older chips to Chinese customers. When the president held a so-called super intelligence luncheon at the White House in late September, Huang was seated directly next to him.

When it came to the mounting blowback against AI, Huang at times acted as if he could talk his way out of it. The leaders of companies including Anthropic and OpenAI made a point of acknowledging the dangers of the technology they were building, but Huang’s views on public policy were almost never in conflict with his company’s business interests. He told a crowd in Paris in October that one way to dampen opposition to data centers was just to describe facilities as AI factories. “Now, as you know, almost nobody likes data centers,” he said. “But everybody likes factories,” he added. “Because factories produce valuable things.”

Huang was clear about his contempt for those who disagreed with him. This tendency was often on display during the recent debate over what threats AI poses to civilization as a whole. This summer a number of AI companies, starting with OpenAI, disclosed that systems they’d built had hacked into websites during prerelease testing, evading protections the companies had put in place to keep them from accessing the internet. The ensuing panic culminated with OpenAI, Anthropic and Musk, whose company is building similar systems, all saying the technology should be slowed down.

Huang was apparently so unbothered by the OpenAI hack that soon afterward, Nvidia agreed to acquire the victim, an AI developer called Hugging Face, for $12.9 billion. Huang bemoaned the tendency of some AI leaders to “scare people with fantasies and mythology and made-up science.” Doing so, he said, was undermining public faith in a technology whose downsides were far less serious than the doomers would have anyone believe.

If there were problems with testing, Huang said, AI companies simply needed to develop better AI tools to run such tests. Nvidia made some of those tools itself, offering them to its customers. The new software, of course, would be powered by Nvidia’s own chips.

The US began significantly limiting exports to China of advanced technologies related to AI during the first Trump administration, then continued through Joe Biden’s presidency. The banned technologies have included Nvidia’s top-flight chips, as well as less powerful products that US officials say the Chinese military could use. Trump returned to office promising continued confrontation, but he’s vacillated between demonizing China and praising it.

Huang has said the company’s data center sales to China are small enough that investors should assume that it has effectively no business in the country. Describing the market as “completely blocked from us,” Huang in October denounced restrictions on chip exports and Western nations’ generalized hostility toward China. “I did my best to inform the policymakers. I think we made a mistake, and this mistake is hard to unwind, but I did my best, and that’s all I could do,” he told Businessweek.

Enforcing these export controls has fallen to the US Department of Commerce, which has investigated multiple entities on suspicion they had facilitated illegal technology transfers to China. Among those was Megaspeed, a neocloud company in Singapore that’s been one of Nvidia’s largest customers in Southeast Asia and that Bloomberg reported was already drawing scrutiny in 2025. Huang had been questioned internally last year about his relationship with Megaspeed founder Alice Huang, who’d been briefly detained in Singapore that year.

When images of the two Huangs (no relation) sharing a drink together began circulating in late October, Huang said their connection was a mundane business relationship. On Oct. 30 he told Bloomberg News that the relationship was “normal and not unusual.” He has played down the importance of the black market trade in Nvidia chips, describing it as trivial, and said Nvidia had determined that Megaspeed wasn’t illegally sending technology to China. “We’ve studied all their deployments of our GPUs and counted every one,” he said in the interview. “The US government also has as well.”

Publicly, there was little movement on the issue until Nov. 6, when the Financial Times reported that the relationship had been romantic. When Nvidia’s general counsel went to Huang’s house and confronted him about it, he finally confessed. Two days later, Huang sent an email to the entire company, admitting to infidelity, apologizing and saying he’d cooperate with the board’s investigation.

At that point, it seemed possible that Huang would be able to hold on to his job. The New York Times’ report several days later that he’d also had an affair with Powell sealed his fate. Huang acknowledged it was over and accepted that he’d done this all to himself, people who have spoken to him told Bloomberg News. He described himself, the people said, as a “piece of s---.”

For the moment the Huang affairs are fully occupying the attention of Silicon Valley. Just about everyone is outraged for different reasons. Some lament that his behavior fits a depressing pattern of bad behavior among powerful men. Others, like Musk, argue that Huang is too important to lose and are petitioning the board to reinstate him.

The shock waves are also reverberating through Washington. Huang agreed to testify at a congressional hearing that will involve the House committees on China, foreign affairs and intelligence. Lawmakers are also looking for answers from Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick and other administration officials about whether they were insufficiently vigilant about keeping advanced Nvidia chips from ending up in China and how Nvidia’s influence may have played a role. On Friday, Secretary of the Treasury Scott Bessent blamed Lutnick for not blacklisting Megaspeed. Megaspeed says it’s conducting an internal investigation and that Alice Huang hasn’t worked there since Aug. 30, 2025. She now resides in her home country, in Beijing with the knowledge of security officials, Bloomberg News reported last week.

That Nvidia’s corporate structure had been shaped to his specifications was always a point of pride for Huang. He said it made the company more flexible and able to keep up with changes in technology. In May 2023, Huang acknowledged to Businessweek that this approach wouldn’t make the business easy to hand over. “Our company was built so that I know how to run it. And so for as long as I’m running it, that’s all that matters,” he said. When asked to consider whether he’d made himself a single point of failure, he didn’t answer directly, arguing instead that companies should be designed with a specific CEO in mind. “You should build a race car for the driver,” he said. “You should not build a generic car for a generic driver.”

On Tuesday, Nvidia will report quarterly earnings for the last full period of Huang’s tenure. Kress will address investors for the first time in her role as interim CEO and will face Nvidia’s staff at an all-hands meeting the next day. Over the course of the week, she will perhaps shed some light on how Nvidia could eventually rebuild its vehicle for whoever takes the wheel. And the race that Huang helped set off will speed on without him. —With Austin Carr
